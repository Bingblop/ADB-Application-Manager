This app can change things that Android normally protects. Almost every change can be undone, and a few cannot. This page tells you which is which, how to work in small safe steps, and what to do if the phone stops behaving.

The idea in a few lines

Android keeps apps apart from each other and from the system on purpose. A working mode lets this app reach across that line, so it can switch off, remove or change things that Android's own Settings will not touch. The Debloater and the batch actions that remove or switch off apps show a list and ask first, and the app keeps a history with Undo for the common changes. But the buttons in one app's menu act at once, and the app cannot know which apps your phone needs. You decide, so it pays to go slowly. Looking never harms anything: reading the app list, permissions, manifests and logs changes nothing. Trouble starts only when you change something.

The four golden rules

  1. Change little. Touch a few things at a time, and start with the safest: apps marked USER, apps the UAD-NG Debloater rates Recommended, a setting you can explain. If you cannot say in one sentence what an app or a setting does, leave it alone. Prefer the gentlest tool: Force Stop, Suspend and Freeze delete nothing, and Suspend and Freeze are undone with one tap. Uninstall and Clear Data are the heavy ones.
  2. Back up first. Copy your photos, contacts and messages somewhere else, the way you normally do. In the app, make a backup of an app before you remove or clear it, and save a profile or a saved list so you remember what you changed. Before you edit a hidden setting, use Copy value to keep the old value. Know your screen lock and Google account password: after a factory reset you will need them.
  3. One change at a time. Change one app, one setting, one overlay or one density step, then look at the phone. If you change five things and something breaks, you will not know which did it. Keep batches short (three to five apps) and read the confirmation list.
  4. Test, then wait. After a change, restart the phone and try what you use every day: a call, a text, the camera, Wi-Fi and mobile data, Bluetooth, the keyboard, unlocking, notifications, an alarm, charging. Live with the change for a day before the next one, because a problem can show up later.
Tip. Freeze first, uninstall later. A frozen app keeps its data, costs one tap to bring back, and shows you whether anything breaks. For a system app, neither action frees much storage (its file stays on the protected system part), so there is rarely a reason to hurry.

If the phone will not start: a boot loop and the recovery ladder

A boot loop is when the phone restarts again and again, or sits on its logo, and never reaches the home screen. It happens when something Android needs at start-up is missing or broken. With this app, removing or disabling a vital system app is the usual cause. It is rare if you follow the four rules, and you will know which few apps to blame.

Risky. If the phone is looping, stop. Do not try random things or keep removing apps. Work down this ladder one step at a time and stop at the first step that works. The steps are in the order of what they cost you.

Before a phone update

A system update replaces parts of Android. It can bring back apps you removed, switch frozen apps on again, and leave you with working modes that must be set up again after the restart.

  • Save what you did. In Profiles, save a profile and tap Watch. After the update the app tells you if apps came back. Review shows what would change, and Confirm apply removes them again. Share sends the profile as a file you can keep somewhere safe.
  • Be careful with Advanced and Expert removals. Nobody can promise how a maker's update copes with apps removed for your user. Before a big update (a new Android version), the cautious choice is to Restore anything removed above the Recommended level, and remove it again once the update works.
  • Back up. Copy your own data off the phone, and keep the app backups you care about (in Download/ADB App Manager/Backups).
  • Charge and free space. Keep the battery above half, preferably plugged in, with several gigabytes free.
  • Expect to set up the working mode again. After the restart ADB over TCP is off, Shizuku has stopped and the Wireless Debugging address may have changed. On a rooted phone an update may remove root: follow your root manager's instructions first.
  • Afterwards, check the Frozen and Bloatware (Uninstalled) counts at the top of the Application Manager, and the card that says apps no longer match your watched profile.

What never to do

  • Do not remove, freeze or clear the data of the parts the phone needs, unless you know the consequences and have a replacement: the system interface (System UI, com.android.systemui), your launcher, the phone and dialer, contacts storage and messages, your keyboard, Settings, the permission controller and package installer, Google Play services and the Play Store (com.android.vending) if you use Google apps, the Android System WebView, and anything for emergency alerts, the SIM, mobile data, Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, fingerprint or face unlock and the camera. Clearing the data of a contacts or messages app deletes what it keeps on the phone.
  • Never remove an app rated Unsafe, and treat a "Needed by" line that names apps you use as a stop sign. Do not use Select shown on a long list you have not read.
  • Do not paste a command you do not understand, even from a friendly website, and do not press Always allow in this chat for a coding agent unless you accept whatever follows.
  • Do not apply a profile, list or command preset from someone else without reading it.
  • Do not switch off the two Block switches in the APK Installer, or tick "Always allow" for an ADB fingerprint you do not recognise.
  • Do not change adb_enabled, adb_wifi_enabled, Wi-Fi or airplane mode in Hidden Settings while the app uses ADB over that connection. You can cut yourself off.
  • Do not delete in system folders (/system, /data, /vendor) with the File Manager, or type extreme density or size numbers.
  • Do not leave a door open. Switch Wireless debugging off when you do not need it, and restart after using ADB over TCP: the port listens on your Wi-Fi while it is open.

When something goes wrong

  1. Stop. Do not make another change.
  2. Undo the last thing you did, using the table above. Read Android's own message first: it is in a Command Result box.
  3. Look in Troubleshooting for the exact words on the screen, or in the questions and answers.
  4. Ask for help with the details collected at the end of Troubleshooting.

The basics

What does this app do?

It lists every app on your phone and lets you freeze, remove, back up and inspect them. It also installs app files, browses files, runs commands, changes Android's hidden settings and manages another Android device such as a watch. All of it runs on the phone, and in the usual case you need no computer. See Welcome.

Is it safe?

The app itself has no ads, no accounts and no analytics (Privacy). What matters is what you do with it. Freezing is easy to undo. Removing system apps and clearing data can hurt if you pick the wrong thing. Follow the four golden rules in Staying safe, start with apps rated Recommended, and change a few things at a time. No guide can promise that a change is safe on every phone.

Is it free?

Yes. The About tab says the app is free and stays free, and this guide describes no paid feature. The Buy me a coffee card is optional and only opens PayPal in your browser. If you use a coding agent with your own key, the provider may bill that account, which is between you and the provider (Coding agents).

Does it need root?

No. Root is one of four working modes, and the quickest if your phone already has it. Wireless Debugging (Android 11 and newer) and ADB over TCP need no root. Only a few things need it, mainly backing up and restoring an app's data (Backups). Without root you can still freeze, suspend, bring back apps and, on most phones, remove system apps for your user (The app menu says what to do when Android refuses). The app cannot root your phone (Root).

Does it need a computer?

Often not. On Android 11 or newer, Wireless Debugging needs only Wi-Fi. On a rooted phone you need nothing. On Android 8 to 10 without root, you need a computer once to switch on ADB over TCP (or to start Shizuku), and again after every restart. The table in Working modes lays it out.

Which versions of Android does it work on?

Android 8.0 or newer. The app will not install on older versions. It declares Android 14 as the version it is built for, and this guide cannot promise that every feature behaves the same on newer versions. Some features need more than 8.0: Wireless Debugging needs Android 11, and the Material You theme needs Android 12.

Will it work on my Samsung, Pixel, Xiaomi or other phone?

It uses Android's own commands, so in principle it works on any phone with Android 8.0 or newer. Makers differ in small ways. On some recent Samsung phones, Auto Blocker can block installs that do not come from a store, and Samsung's own apps are checked through the Galaxy Store. On Xiaomi, Redmi and POCO, changes to hidden settings and themes may need USB debugging (Security settings) switched on in Developer options. Where you find Build number differs by maker (Wireless Debugging, step 1). The app has not been tried on every phone. If something misbehaves on yours, report it (Troubleshooting).

Will it void my warranty?

It depends on the phone maker and on your country, and nobody here can promise either way. Freezing, suspending and removing apps for your user go through Android's own commands. The app has no feature that unlocks the bootloader or flashes firmware. Rooting is different: the guide says it usually voids the warranty, and on Samsung phones it trips the Knox counter (Root). Check your maker's terms. Before a repair, undo what you can (Enable, Restore); a factory reset also puts back system apps you removed for your user.

Can it brick my phone?

The app has no feature that flashes firmware or unlocks the bootloader. But removing or disabling a vital system app can leave the phone in a boot loop, and a factory reset normally cures that (Staying safe). The Terminal, above all as root, can run any command, including harmful ones. That is why the guide asks you never to paste a command you do not understand.

Why do I have to switch on Developer options?

Android keeps ADB, USB debugging and Wireless debugging in a hidden menu, so people who do not need them do not stumble on them. Tapping Build number seven times makes the menu appear, and every option inside stays off until you switch it on (Wireless Debugging, step 1).

Working modes and connections

What is a working mode, and which one should I pick?

It is the way the app borrows enough power to change other apps: ADB over TCP, Wireless Debugging, Shizuku or Root, or Auto, which picks the first that is ready. Rooted phone: pick Root. Android 11 or newer, not rooted: Wireless Debugging. Android 8 to 10, not rooted: ADB over TCP with a computer once, or Shizuku. Working modes has the full decision list.

What is the difference between ADB over TCP and Wireless Debugging?

Both let the app talk to the phone's ADB service. Wireless Debugging is Android's own Wi-Fi feature (Android 11 and newer): you pair once with a code, and the connect port changes whenever it is switched off and on. ADB over TCP uses the fixed port 5555 and does not depend on the Wi-Fi once it is on, but a restart switches it off, and on Android 8 to 10 you need a computer to switch it on (ADB over TCP).

Why does the Wireless Debugging address keep changing?

Android picks a new random connect port each time Wireless debugging is switched off and on, and it often switches it off itself when you leave the Wi-Fi or restart the phone. The pairing stays, so you only read the new IP address & Port and tap Connect. To avoid this, you can switch on ADB over TCP from Wireless Debugging with one button, until the next restart (Wireless Debugging).

Does it keep working after a restart?

It depends on the mode. Root: yes, nothing to redo. Shizuku: it stops, so start it again, unless it was started with root and set to start itself. ADB over TCP: switched off again, so repeat the one-time step. Wireless Debugging: the pairing is kept, but you switch it on again and connect to the new port. A freeze, a suspend or a removal is stored by Android and not by this app, so it normally stays after a restart (a system update can undo it).

Is it safe to leave Wireless debugging or ADB over TCP switched on?

Switch them off when you do not need them. While port 5555 is open, the phone listens on the Wi-Fi, and another device on the same network could try to connect. It would still need your approval in the "Allow USB debugging?" question, so never tick "Always allow" for a fingerprint you do not recognise. A restart switches ADB over TCP off (ADB over TCP).

Freezing, removing and getting apps back

What is the difference between freezing, suspending and uninstalling?

Freeze (Android calls it disabling) keeps the app installed with its data, but it cannot run and disappears from your launcher. Enable undoes it. Suspend keeps the app enabled but paused: its icon is greyed out and it cannot be opened until you Unsuspend. Uninstall on a system app removes it for your user only and deletes its data for you; the file stays in the system, so Reinstall can bring it back. On an app you installed, uninstalling removes it for good. When unsure, freeze (Words you will see, Application Manager).

Can I get an app back?

Usually. A frozen app: Enable. A suspended app: Unsuspend. A system app you removed: Reinstall, Restore in the Debloater, or Undo in the History. Such apps stay in the list with the UNINSTALLED badge so you can find them. A factory reset also restores them. An app you installed and removed must be installed again from a store or a file. Data you cleared is gone unless you have a backup that included it (that needs Root).

Can I undo everything at once?

There is no "undo all" button. Undo works one History entry or one batch at a time (the History keeps the last 150 entries), and the hidden settings have their own list of changes to revert. A saved profile lets you apply a known state again. The only all-in-one undo is a factory reset, and that also erases your own data.

Which apps are safe to remove?

No list can say that for every phone. The UAD-NG Debloater shows a community rating for each app (Recommended, Advanced, Expert or Unsafe) and describes it. Start with Recommended, read each description, change a few apps at a time, and never remove one rated Unsafe. The ratings are advice, not a promise (Debloating safely).

Will it free up storage, save battery or make my phone faster?

Not by much for system apps: their file stays on the protected system part, so little space is freed. A frozen app does not run in the background, which can cut background activity, but whether you notice depends on the apps. Removing an app you installed does free its space, and Trim Caches in Settings clears the temporary files of every app, which frees some space too (see the next question). Do not expect miracles.

Does Trim Caches delete my data?

No. Trim Caches, the last card of Settings (the gear), deletes only cache: the temporary files each app keeps in its own cache folders, such as thumbnails, saved web pages, map tiles and buffered video. It does not touch apps, their data and settings, logins and accounts, saved games, photos, videos, downloads or your files, and nothing is uninstalled. Afterwards an app may start a little slower and may fetch images again over mobile data, and an app that keeps offline downloads in its cache (some music, video and map apps) may need to download them again. It needs a working mode, and it cannot be undone, though apps simply rebuild what they need (Free up space by clearing every app's cache, Settings).

Will a system update undo my changes?

It can. A big Android or phone-maker update can bring back apps you removed and switch frozen apps on again, and this differs from phone to phone. Save a profile and tap Watch: after an update the app tells you what came back and lets you review and remove it again (Profiles, and the section "Before a phone update" in Staying safe).

Why do some apps come back?

Most often because of a system update, as above. A factory reset restores system apps removed for your user, and Reinstall, Restore, Undo or restoring a backup brings one back on purpose. If an app returns with none of these, phones differ and this guide cannot cover every maker's software. Remove it again, and consider watching a profile.

Installing and updating

What are APKS, APKM and XAPK?

They are bundles of several app files that belong together: a base file plus small parts for your processor, screen density and language. The APK Installer opens all of them and ticks the parts that fit your phone. XAPK may also carry game data (OBB), which is copied after the install (Split packages, APKS, APKM, XAPK and game data).

What is the 32-bit APK, and which download do I need?

Each release has three APK files: arm64-v8a for 64-bit phones (nearly all), armeabi-v7a for 32-bit phones, and universal, which carries both. A small SHA256SUMS.txt file lists their checksums. If you are not sure, take the universal one. The difference is the small ADB program inside the app. The 32-bit build is new and has been tried on far fewer phones (Installing and updating the app).

How do I update the app?

Install the new APK over the old one: your settings, lists and profiles are kept. Or open About, tap Check for update, then Update now. Either way the new file must be this app, newer, and signed with the same key (Installing and updating the app).

Can I install apps from Google Play with it?

No. The app does not download from Google Play. It installs app files you give it, and the App Stores tab browses four open-source catalogs (ShizuStore, GitHub, F-Droid and Orion) and installs from them. Play Store apps keep updating through the Play Store or Aurora Store; the App Updater only points you there.

Can I move the app to the SD card?

No. By default Android keeps an app in internal storage unless the app allows otherwise, and this app does not ask for that. Your own files are different: the File Manager can add an SD card or a USB drive, and you can move backups and downloads there with Copy and Paste.

Can I use it on a watch?

Yes. The Connected Devices tab manages a Wear OS watch from your phone over adb (the developer tool, see the glossary), mostly through Wi-Fi, and also through a USB cable or a watch's Bluetooth link: apps, installs, a console, the log, files, hidden settings and screen density. The short version: no account, no ads, no analytics and no server of the developer's. Everything runs on your phone, and the app goes online only for something you open or ask for. This page lists every contact and says where your data lives and how to delete it.

In short

  • No account and no sign-up.
  • No ads, no analytics, no crash reporting. The libraries shipped with the app (named in Credits) are for archives, text patterns, general helpers, Shizuku and logging.
  • Nothing phones home in the background. The app has no timer that contacts a server. Every contact below starts when you open a tab, press a button or send a chat message.   • The keys the app makes (the ADB key and the APK-signing key) are created on your phone and never leave it. No key is shipped inside the app: every install makes its own.
  • Android's own backup is switched off for the app, so nothing of it goes to a cloud backup.

How to delete everything

  1. Undo what the app changed in Android first. Frozen apps, removed system apps, hidden settings, overlays and themes are stored by Android, not by this app, and they stay when the app goes. Use Enable, Restore, Changes, Revert and Default (Staying safe).
  2. Remove secrets inside the app. Forget the key for each coding agent, Clear the conversation, and empty the VirusTotal key and GitHub token boxes.
  3. Delete the files you saved. Open Download/ADB App Manager in the File Manager and delete what you do not want.
  4. Clear the app's storage, or uninstall it. In Android's Settings, Apps, ADB Application Manager Pro, Storage, tap Clear storage: this deletes everything in the app's private storage listed above. Uninstalling also removes its Keystore keys.
  5. Take back the trust you gave. In Developer options tap Revoke USB debugging authorizations, and remove the pairing under Wireless debugging. Remove the app from Shizuku's and your root manager's lists, and switch off its special accesses (such as All files access and Install unknown apps) in Android's Settings.
  6. Outside the phone. A provider keeps what you sent it, under its own rules. Delete your chats there and revoke the API keys on its site.

The About tab and the project's README give its licence as GPL-3.0. The list is not bundled in the app: it is downloaded when you ask for it (Privacy). The advice is that project's, and the UAD-NG Wiki button opens its wiki.

Shizuku

Shell access through Shizuku uses the Shizuku API by RikkaApps. The app ships the Shizuku API libraries (version 13.1.5) and two files named rish and rish_shizuku.dex, which it keeps as a fallback way to run commands through Shizuku. The Shizuku app itself is a separate project, not made by this app's developer (Shizuku).

The Task Manager tab

The Task Manager tab is modeled on RohitKushvaha01/TaskManager on GitHub, as the About tab says.

The 32-bit adb

The 32-bit and universal APKs carry the adb client of Termux's android-tools 37.0.0-2 for arm (from packages.termux.dev), with the 56 shared libraries it needs, taken from the same repository: protobuf 35.1, abseil 20260526.0, libc++ 30, brotli 1.2.0, fmt 11.2.0, lz4 1.10.0, zstd 1.5.7 and zlib 1.3.2. Libraries inside the app

The libraries the app is built with are commons-compress 1.27.1, commons-io 2.16.1, commons-lang3 3.16.0, aircompressor 0.27, xz 1.10, junrar 7.5.5, re2j 1.8, slf4j-api 1.7.36, and the four Shizuku libraries above (shizuku-api, shizuku-aidl, shizuku-provider and shizuku-shared, all 13.1.5). The project's notes name them Apache Commons Compress, Commons IO and Commons Lang3, Tukaani XZ, Aircompressor, junrar, RE2J and the SLF4J API. Commons Compress and its helpers (Commons IO, Commons Lang3, XZ and the SLF4J logging interface) handle 7z and tar archives, junrar reads rar archives, Aircompressor handles zstd, and RE2J matches text patterns that come from catalog data, in a safe way. The app's own files do not repeat their licences. Each project states its own.

Techniques borrowed

The code notes that its fallback for removing a stubborn system app uses the same technique as the App Manager and Canta apps. It also notes that launching a hidden screen is how Activity Manager does it over Shizuku, a technique that dedicated activity launchers use.

Design

The app's looks follow Google's Material 3 and Material You (the colours Android builds from your wallpaper), with Material 3 Expressive motion (Themes and colours).
