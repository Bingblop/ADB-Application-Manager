Android keeps hundreds of small settings that its own Settings app never shows. This tab lists them, lets you read any one, change it, add your own or delete it, and puts the old value back when you change your mind.

Where to find it: the tab named Hidden Settings ("Hidden" on the bottom bar, two lines on the tab bar: Hidden and Settings), right after Live Logcat and right before the Extra Settings tab in the default order (RRO/Monet Customization comes after Extra Settings). If it is missing, it has been switched off: see Turning tabs on and off and moving them.

Looking for Private DNS? It is not in this tab, which edits Android's own settings tables. It is in the Extra Settings tab next to this one: a small note at the top of this tab has a button that opens it.

Before you start

  • You need a working mode. The app reads and changes these settings by running Android's own settings command through your working mode (ADB over TCP, Wireless Debugging, Shizuku or Root; see Working modes and the glossary). In Read-Only mode the tab cannot even show the list. With no mode ready, a card says Needs ADB, Wireless Debugging, Shizuku or Root and its Set Up button opens Working Modes. Connect one, come back, and the list loads.
  • No computer is needed, and the tab does not use the app's own "Write Secure Settings" permission (see App permissions). What you can change is decided by the mode and by Android.
  • Which settings exist depends on your Android version and phone maker. One your phone does not have is not in the list.
Risky. These are the settings Android reads while it runs. A wrong value can cut the connection this app uses, lock you out of the keyboard or screen, or upset the system. The app marks the worst ones careful and asks first, but others can also cause trouble. If you do not know what a setting does, leave it alone. Before you change one, tap its row and use Copy value to keep the original in a note. Every change you make can be put back (see Undoing settings changes), but a lock-out can stop you reaching the app, so read "If you lose the connection" below first.

The three tables

A setting is a name with a value, such as screen_off_timeout with 60000. Android keeps them in three groups called tables, and the tab has one sub-tab for each.

TableWhat lives thereExamples
GlobalSettings for the whole phone.adb_enabled, airplane_mode_on, window_animation_scale, device_name
SecureSettings of the current user that only the system, or the shell, may change: keyboards, accessibility, location mode.default_input_method, location_mode, ui_night_mode
SystemThe classic per-user settings: brightness, screen timeout, text size, rotation, volumes.screen_off_timeout, font_scale, volume_music

Android reads many settings only at certain moments, and some phone makers overwrite a few with their own, so a change that "does nothing" is not always a fault.

What the screen shows

  • Global, Secure, System (three wide buttons) choose the table. The number under each name is its count of settings: an ellipsis while loading, an exclamation mark if it could not be read, and "matches / total" while a search or filter is on.
  • Search settings... (the box, with an x to clear it), ＋ (create a setting) and ⋯ (More: sort order, reload, your changes, copy and share).
  • Four chips: All; Switches (values such as 1 or 0, true or false, on or off, yes or no); Described (every setting the app can describe: about 1,100 of Android's own; see "What a setting does and what its values mean" below); Edited (the ones you changed with this app).
  • A status line: the count, the time it was read, and a Refresh link.
  • The list. Each row has the name, what the setting does and which values it takes when the app knows them, and the value (long values are cut after two lines). A dot after the name means you changed it. An orange edge and the word "careful" mark a risky one. (empty) means nothing is stored, (blank) only spaces. A switch at the right means the value is switch-like. The list shows 120 rows at a time; more load as you scroll or with Show more.

What a setting does and what its values mean

Under the name of each setting the list says what it does, and below that Values: which values it takes. The value the setting holds now is in bold in that line, so Values: 0 = off; 1 = priority interruptions only; 2 = total silence; 3 = alarms only tells you at a glance that Do Not Disturb is off. Tap a row and the sheet has an About box with the same, one value to a line, and a Now: line that follows what you type (type 2 and it says Now: 2 = total silence).

  • Where the text comes from. The app knows what about 1,100 of Android's own settings do (the Global, Secure and System tables of Android's source code). The ones people meet most often have been written out in plain words; the others carry the short text from Android's documentation.
  • Reading the values. 0 = off; 1 = on is a switch. A time is in milliseconds (1000 is one second, 60000 one minute). A decimal such as 1.0 is a scale: 1.0 normal, 0.5 half, 2.0 double. percent, Hz (refresh rate) and kelvin (colour warmth) are units. A list uses , or : between its items; package/class is an app component.
  • Only real switches flip. A setting the app knows takes more than 0 and 1 (for example wifi_on, zen_mode) is never flipped by a press and hold: tap it and pick one of its values.
  • The search also looks in the values text, so milliseconds or total silence finds settings.
  • A setting without a description. Phone makers and apps add many settings of their own (a name starting sec_ often comes from Samsung, miui_ from Xiaomi, a name like com.example.app.key from that app). For these the editor says What it probably is and guesses from the name and the value: an on/off switch, a time in milliseconds, a web address, a package name. It is only a guess; if you cannot tell what a setting does, leave it alone.
  • The How to read this list link under the title of the tab opens a short explanation of the three tables, a row and the kinds of value. The ? next to the title opens this guide topic.

Find a setting

  1. Open Hidden Settings. Grey bars show while the tables are read; then each button shows its count.
  2. Tap a table, or just search: the counts on all three buttons change to "matches / total".
  3. Type in the search box. It looks in the name, the value and the description, ignores capitals, and every word you type must match. timeout finds screen_off_timeout.
  4. Narrow the list with a chip, or tap ⋯ and choose Sort by: Name A-Z (default), Name Z-A or Value.

What you will see: the list shrinks to the matches, with the matching letters highlighted. If nothing matches, the page says Nothing matches "text" in Global. and offers Create "text" in Global (only when what you typed is one word that can be a setting name) and Show everything. Back clears the search first. The table, filter and sort order you used last are remembered; the search text is not.

Read a value

The value is on the row. To see all of it, tap the row: a sheet opens with the value in a box. The app also asks the phone for the real value while it opens, because the list may be a little old; if it differs, the box is updated and a message says Showing the value the phone holds now. The sheet's Reload link does the same on demand.

Change a value

  1. Tap the row. A sheet slides up with the name, the table, the description and, for a risky setting, an orange box explaining the danger. Read it.
  2. Type in the Value box, or tap a quick value. If the setting is documented, its own values come first, each with its meaning (for example 2 · total silence); then 0, 1, true, false, null, (empty), -1. A line under the box says what kind of value it looks like and what it was before.
  3. A red line means it cannot be saved yet. The limits are 20,000 characters, no control characters (invisible special codes, apart from a tab or a line break), and a size that fits in one command sent through ADB (about 62,000 bytes once quoted). Save stays grey until the problem is gone.
  4. Tap Save. For a careful setting your phone first asks you to confirm.

What you will see: the sheet stays open while the phone applies the change, so a refusal never loses what you typed. On success it closes, the row shows the new value with a dot, and an Undo bar appears for 3 seconds. Saving the same value says No change. The word null is stored as that text; use (empty) for nothing and Delete to remove the setting. The sheet's other links: Web search (looks the setting up on the web by its table and name), Ask agent (asks your Default Ask Agent what the setting is and whether it is safe to change; the setting's name, table and current value are sent to that agent's company, only when you tap the button, and many settings belong to the phone maker, so the answer may be a careful guess; until the agent has its key, or with No default chosen, the built-in web lookup answers instead), Copy name, Copy value and Copy command (the exact settings put line, to paste in the ADB Shell of the Console tab).

Flip a switch

  1. Tap the switch at the end of a row, or press and hold the row for about half a second (you feel a short tap).
  2. Confirm if it is a careful setting.
  3. The switch moves and the Undo bar says, for example, show_touches: 0 → 1.

Capitals are kept (True becomes False). Settings that look like switches but are really a choice among several values, such as zen_mode, ui_night_mode or screen_off_timeout, have no switch (holding one says Not a switch: tap it to edit). In the editor, a switch-like setting has a Flip button that only changes the box; you still tap Save.

Add a new setting

Android uses a setting only if something reads it by that exact name, so an invented one does nothing. Add settings you have a real name for, from a guide or an app's documentation.

  1. Tap ＋. A sheet titled New setting opens.
  2. Choose Global, Secure or System (it starts on the table you were in).
  3. Type the Name: up to 256 characters, not starting with a dash, with no equals sign, spaces or control characters. A red line says what is wrong.
  4. Type the Value, or leave it empty to create the setting with nothing in it.
  5. Tap ＋ Create. If the name exists, the button reads Replace, a note shows the current value, and you must confirm. The app asks the phone first, so a name missing from an old list is still caught.

What you will see: the list jumps to that table with the name in the search box, and the Undo bar says "＋ Created name". Undo deletes it again.

Delete a setting

  1. Tap the row, then Delete (red, at the bottom of the sheet).
  2. Confirm. The box says Android and apps then behave as if it was never set.
  3. The row disappears and the Undo bar says "Deleted name". Undo brings it back with its old value.

If you lose the connection

If you switched off adb_enabled or adb_wifi_enabled (or Wi-Fi, or switched on airplane mode) while the app was using ADB, the app can no longer reach the phone to change them back. Open your phone's own Settings, go to Developer options (a hidden part of Android's Settings; if you cannot find it, search the Settings for its name), switch USB debugging or Wireless debugging back on (and Wi-Fi), then reconnect in Working Modes. If another mode (Shizuku or Root) still works, Revert the entry in Changes instead. If a keyboard or accessibility setting locks you out, use your phone's own Settings to choose a working keyboard and switch accessibility services back, then revert the entry.

Hidden Settings keeps two ways back: an Undo bar that appears right after a change, and a history called Changes that keeps your last 100 changes, between launches, so each one can be reviewed and reverted later.

Where to find it: the Undo bar appears at the bottom of the screen in the Hidden Settings and RRO/Monet Customization tabs. The history opens from the clock button next to the search box of Hidden Settings, or from the Changes button under ⋯.

Before you start

  • Putting a value back is a change like any other, so it needs a working mode (see Working modes).
  • The history only knows about changes made with this app. If another app, Android itself or a computer changed a setting, it is not listed, and the app cannot tell you what the old value was.
  • The page has no backup and no restore for settings. There is no button that saves a whole table to a file or loads one back. The history is the safety net, and the section "Keep your own copy" below shows a manual way to save a list before you experiment.

The Changes history

  1. Open Hidden Settings and tap the clock button next to the search box (or tap ⋯, then Changes).
  2. A sheet titled Changes opens, with the line "The last 100 changes made with this app, newest first, kept between launches. Revert puts the old value back."
  3. Each entry shows the setting name, the table, how long ago and the date and time (for example "Global · 3 min ago · 15 Oct, 21:30"), and the change as old value → new value. A value that was not there before shows as (not set); an empty one as (empty).
  4. Tap Revert under the entry you want to undo.

When a 101st change is made, the oldest entry is dropped. If nothing has been changed yet, the sheet says Nothing was changed with this app yet. The same history is behind the Edited filter chip and the small dot after the name of every changed setting in the list.

Revert one change

  1. In Changes, find the entry and tap Revert.
  2. If the setting has changed again since (by you, by Android, or by another app), a box says name has changed since: it is now ... (this change left it ...). Put the old value back anyway? Choose OK only if you want the old value back regardless.
  3. If the setting is marked careful, you are asked to confirm once more (see Hidden Settings).
  4. The sheet closes and a message says Put back: name (or Removed: name when the entry was a setting you created, so there is nothing to put back).

Reverting is recorded as a new change, so it appears at the top of the history and can itself be reverted.

If the old value was longer than 2,000 characters, the entry shows (too long to keep) in its place and has no Revert button, because the old text was not stored. For those, use the Undo bar right after the change, or fix the setting by hand.

Revert many changes

There is no "revert all" button. Revert the entries one at a time, starting from the top (the newest), so that every entry finds the setting in the state it left it. Going out of order is allowed, but you will get the "has changed since" question for entries that depend on a later one.

  1. Open ⋯ → Changes.
  2. Tap Revert on the first (newest) entry. The sheet closes after each revert.
  3. Open ⋯ → Changes again and repeat. Check your phone from time to time, so you stop as soon as it behaves normally.

What is kept, and where

  • The last 150 changes. Older ones are forgotten as new ones arrive.
  • Where: inside this app's own storage on the phone. The app does not write a file to Downloads or anywhere else, and nothing is sent to anyone. The history survives closing the app and restarting the phone. Because it lives in the app's own storage, clearing the app's data or uninstalling the app removes it (and with it your way back).
  • Theme changes too. A Material You theme change made in the RRO/Monet tab is a change of the secure setting theme_customization_overlay_packages, and is listed here with that name. Reverting it puts the old theme setting back. Overlay on/off changes are not listed in Changes, only in the Undo bar.
  • What is in each entry: the table, the name, when it happened, the old value and the new value.

Clear the history

At the bottom of the Changes sheet, Clear history asks Forget the history of changes? The settings themselves stay as they are. If you confirm, the list is emptied, the small dots and the Edited filter forget everything, and nothing on the phone changes. You cannot get the entries back, so do this only when you are happy with how the phone behaves. The button only shows while there is something to clear.

Back up the changes you made, and restore them

Under the list in the Changes sheet there are two more buttons next to Clear history.

  • Back up changes opens Android's share sheet with a small file, hidden_settings_(date).json. It holds every setting you changed with this app: the table, the name, the value it has now, and the value it had before. Save it to Drive, send it to yourself, or keep it with your other backups. Settings whose value was too long to keep are left out.
  • Restore… asks you to pick such a file. The app checks it (a name with spaces or strange characters, an unknown table or a value that is not text is left out), compares it with what the phone holds now, and asks Put N hidden settings back from the backup?, naming the first few and saying how many are marked risky. Settings that already match are skipped. If you agree, the values are put back one after the other (a value of removed deletes the setting), the progress is shown as Restoring 3 of 12…, and one message at the end says how many worked and which did not. Every restored setting is written to the history again, so it can be reverted.

The file only knows the settings you changed here, not the whole phone, and it is for the same kind of device: a setting that another phone does not have is created, which it may ignore. A backup is not a reset button for settings the app never touched; for those, use the copy described next. Needs a working mode, like any change.

Keep your own copy before you experiment

The history cannot restore a whole table, and it does not know settings you did not change. If you are about to try several things, save the original values first.

  1. Open Hidden Settings and choose the table (Global, Secure or System).
  2. Clear the search box and tap All, so that every setting is shown.
  3. Tap ⋯, then Copy shown (a message says Copied 340 settings or similar), and paste into a notes app. Or tap Share shown and send the text to yourself.
  4. Do the same for the other tables if you want to.

The copy is plain text with one name=value line per setting. To restore something later, find the line and set the value by hand (tap the row, type, Save), or create the setting with ＋ if it was deleted. That text cannot be read back in by the app; the file from Back up changes above can.

Careful. The lists can hold personal details, such as the device name, the Bluetooth name and address, and the Android ID. Keep the copy private. If a very long list does not go through the share sheet, narrow it with the search or a filter first.

If something goes wrong

  • Revert says it could not change the setting. A sheet shows Android's own answer, the same as for any change. Read it; see "If a change is refused or nothing happens" in Hidden Settings.
  • The history is empty but you know you changed things. The changes were made outside this app, or the app's data was cleared since. Set the values back by hand from your own copy.
  • The phone is unusable after a change and you cannot reach the app. Use your phone's own Settings (Developer options, language and input, accessibility) to undo what you can, reconnect in Working Modes, then revert the entry. See also Safety.

Where to find it: the tab named Extra Settings (two lines on the tab bar: Extra and Settings), right after Hidden Settings in the default order. The top of the tab

Where it is: the Extra Settings tab (right after Hidden Settings on the tab bar), first section, Private DNS. It is not in the Hidden Settings tab, the one that edits Android's own settings, and it is no longer a card in the gear Settings page (that page has a small pointer to it).

This section changes the phone, so applying a service needs a working mode (ADB, Wireless Debugging, Shizuku or Root). Test speed

Tap Test speed. The app tries every service in the list (and the host name typed under Another host name, if there is one) and fills in each tile as soon as its answer comes in, in any order. A bar fills the button and the line under it says Testing… 5 of 15; Stop ends the test (the answers that came in stay, the others say Not tested). Leaving the tab also stops it. Starting a new test replaces the old one.

Each tile shows the time in milliseconds in all, and under it the three parts: connect (the network connection to port 853, the closest thing to a ping), TLS (the secure handshake) and answer (one real DNS lookup over that connection). Each is the middle value of three tries; if fewer than three worked, the tile says so. A tile whose service did not work says why: Name not found, No answer on 853, Certificate error, No DNS answer or Timed out. A tile that says Couldn’t test now only means the phone had too many name lookups waiting: that says nothing against the service, so test again.

When all answers are in, the quickest working service gets a Fastest pill and a bright outline, and the line under the button says which it is (Fastest on this network). The result is a measurement from this phone's network only, at this moment; a different Wi-Fi or mobile data can give another order. Sort by speed (off by default) puts the answers in speed order, quickest first, then the services not tested, then the ones that failed; off, the list keeps its usual order.

Privacy. Your phone first looks up each service's name with the DNS it uses now (once). Then each service may receive up to three short encrypted connections, each asking for example.com. See which Doze phase the phone is in, push it through the phases to test how an app behaves when the phone sleeps, and choose a power profile.

Where it is: the Extra Settings tab, the section Doze & power governor. Reading and changing needs a working mode (ADB, Wireless Debugging, Shizuku or Root); without one the section says so and its buttons are off.

The timeline

Doze is Android's deep sleep (a glossary word: the phone stops most background work when it lies still with the screen off). The timeline shows five phases, Active, Inactive, Idle pending, Idle (deep) and Maintenance, with the phone's current phase lit and the exact state Android reports under it (for example SENSING under Idle pending). A row of small chips below shows the state of Light Doze, the lighter version that starts a few minutes after the screen goes off. Both come from dumpsys deviceidle get deep and get light, read when the tab opens and after every action; Check again reads them again.

Actions

  • Force deep Doze: puts the phone into deep Doze now (dumpsys deviceidle force-idle deep). Most apps lose background work and network until the next maintenance window; the phone still rings. It stays forced until you wake the device or the phone restarts.
  • Step to next phase: moves Doze one phase forward (dumpsys deviceidle step deep).
  • Wake device: ends a forced Doze and lets Android decide again (dumpsys deviceidle unforce).
  • Simulate motion: tells Doze the phone was picked up (dumpsys deviceidle motion); a Doze that Android entered on its own ends, a forced one does not.

Each action opens the confirm sheet with one sentence on what it does and the command; after it runs, the section reads the phase back from the phone and shows that, with the command's own words under it. A phone that refuses (for example Unable to go deep idle; not enabled) is reported as such. The Motion sensor line says what the phone's own Doze report says (whether Doze uses the sensor, whether it is listening now, the sensor's name); a phone that does not print those lines reads as state unknown. Android has no switch for that sensor, so the section does not offer one.

Power profiles

Three cards: Performance (fixed performance mode on, battery saver off), Balanced (battery saver off, adaptive saver on: Android's default) and Ultra saver (battery saver on). A tap opens the sheet with the commands; Apply runs them through the working mode (settings put global low_power 0 or 1 through the same path as Hidden Settings, and cmd power set-fixed-performance-mode-enabled / set-adaptive-power-saver-enabled where the phone knows them). Most phones do not report that state; the section then says so and keeps Balanced marked. On Android 10 and older the Performance card is locked with Not on this Android version.

How much RAM the phone's compressed swap (zram) gives back, and buttons that free the memory caches hold.

Where it is: the Extra Settings tab, the last section, Zram & memory. Reading needs a working mode (ADB, Wireless Debugging, Shizuku or Root).

The numbers

Zram is a piece of RAM that works as compressed swap: when memory runs low Android squeezes the memory of idle apps into it instead of closing them. The badge at the top says the compression, for example 3.0x compression; the bar under it shows what the zram disk holds: Compressed in RAM, RAM saved and Free swap. Four boxes give Allocated (the size of the zram disk), Compressed in RAM, Uncompressed data and RAM saved (uncompressed data minus the RAM the compressed copy takes). They come from /sys/block/zram0/mm_stat, /sys/block/zram0/disksize and /proc/meminfo, read every 5 seconds while the tab is on the screen (the timer stops when you leave the tab or the app goes to the background); the round button reads at once. A phone without zram, or one whose shell may not read the counters, shows Not available with what the phone said.

The buttons

  • Reclaim disk caches (sync; echo 3 > /proc/sys/vm/drop_caches): writes pending data, then frees the page, folder and inode caches. Root mode only.
  • Drop page caches (sync; echo 1 > /proc/sys/vm/drop_caches): frees only the page cache. Root mode only.
  • Trim app caches: the same as Settings > Trim Caches in All Applications (pm trim-caches 128G), works in every working mode; the result opens in its own sheet.

Each button opens the confirm sheet; nothing runs before you confirm. After an action the numbers are read again and the change is shown in the sheet and under each box (for example -95 MB under Compressed in RAM). In ADB, Wireless Debugging and Shizuku mode the two root buttons are locked with Needs Root mode; they are never hidden.

Freed cache does not stay freed. The kernel keeps those caches because they make the phone faster, and fills them again as apps read files. Not tried on a phone yet.

What happens to a tab you switch off:

  • It leaves the bars and More at once (a pinned tab gives up its place on the bottom bar), and Back no longer goes to it.
  • Anything inside the app that points at it does not open it. A message says, for example, APK Installer/Updater is turned off. Turn it on in Settings, Feature List. and nothing else happens: no sheet closes and no download starts.   • You can switch off every one of the fourteen if you like. Only Application Manager and About are then left.

Get a tab back

  1. Open Settings with the gear icon and scroll to Feature List.
  2. Turn the tab's switch on. It returns in the position its row has in the list.
  3. If you cannot remember which tab you hid, or want everything as it was, tap Reset to Default.

Move a tab

  1. In the Feature List, tap the up arrow on a row to move that tab one place earlier, or the down arrow to move it one place later.
  2. The row moves and the bars follow at once. The screen scrolls so the row stays under your thumb, so you can tap the same arrow again to keep moving it.
  3. Repeat for other tabs until the order suits you.

A tab that is switched off still keeps its place in the list. If you switch it on again later, it returns to that place. Moving a tab never changes the position of Application Manager or About.

Reset to Default

The Reset to Default button under the list switches all fourteen tabs on, puts them back in the default order shown above and pins the four default tabs again (the shortcut menus stay as you set them). A message says Tabs are back in their default order, all turned on. There is no question first, and it does not touch anything else in Settings.

When the app is updated

Your choice is checked against the tabs the new version has. A tab that no longer exists is dropped from your choice. A tab the update adds is placed after the tab that comes before it in the default order, so your saved order keeps working. Changes of this kind: the Command-Line Interface tab became Console and a Terminal tab, with the log inside Console. Since v7.14 the Console holds the ADB Shell and the Terminal as its two parts, and Live Logcat is a tab of its own again, in the place the Terminal tab had. A saved choice keeps its place: Console takes the place the Command-Line Interface had, and is switched off if the Command-Line Interface was switched off.

Tip. If you use only a few tabs, hide the rest and put the ones you use most right after Application Manager. Pin the four you use most so they are one tap away on the bottom bar.

How the app chooses its colours

Four things work together, in this order:

  1. The mode: light or dark. Appearance decides which one is in use now.
  2. The palette: one of the eight cards, or a palette style (Tonal Spot, Vibrant, Fidelity, Content, Neutral, Expressive, Fruit Salad, Rainbow or Monotone) built from the source colour. Either way there is a dark version and a light version.
  3. Your tweaks: any colours you changed with the colour pickers. They are saved for one mode only, so a tweak made in dark mode does not change light mode.
  4. Pure black: if it is on, then in dark mode the backgrounds become black whatever the palette and your tweaks say.

The text colour on buttons is chosen automatically: black on the accent colour wherever black is easy to read (a contrast of 4.5:1), white only where it is not. The fixed colours of Uninstall (red), Freeze (ice blue) and Reinstall (green) always have black text. The phone's status bar takes the colour of the app's background.
